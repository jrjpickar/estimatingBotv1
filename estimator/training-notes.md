# Training notes from real estimating sessions

Lessons taken from A2Z estimators talking through real bids. Read this before every estimate. Each lesson says what to do; the examples below show where it came from.

## General lessons

### Scope
- **Check the reflected ceiling plan (RCP) every time.** On the Montebello job the ceiling demo was missed at first and only caught on the RCP. If the plan set has an RCP or ceiling demo sheet, compare it to the takeoff the same way as the floor demo plan.
- **Ceiling tiles are not the grid.** "Remove ACT ceiling tiles" and "remove ceiling grid" are separate scope items. Ask which one the plans call for, and when the grid stays, say so in the scope line ("leave existing ceiling grid in place").
- **List ceiling devices separately and ask for counts.** Lights, grilles/diffusers, speakers, security devices, sensors and other ceiling-mounted items each need a count or a "by others" decision. If a device name is unclear (the transcript had "motion ejectors/injectors"), ask what it is and whether it is in scope. Do not guess.
- **Small millwork can be noted without changing price.** A single small cabinet (about 24") was judged minor. Still list it in the scope lines so it is covered.
- **Access affects labor and hauling.** A single man door (no roll-up or storefront access) means hand-carrying all debris and more handling time. Flag access as a Condition and ask the estimator how to allow for it.
- **Ceiling height decides the lift.** Ask for the actual ceiling height before picking a scissor lift size. A 15 ft lift was used for a standard strip-mall ceiling.

### Work sequence for the proposal
Write interior demo scope lines in the order the crew will do the work, so debris never lands on flooring that still has to come out:
1. Remove millwork.
2. Remove carpet tile and rubber base (early, while the floor is still clean).
3. Remove doors and walls.
4. Remove ACT ceiling tiles, then lights, grilles/diffusers, speakers, security devices and other confirmed ceiling items.
5. State what stays (for example the ceiling grid).

### Cost build-up
- **Every cost is its own line.** Turn crew size, days, trucks, fuel, equipment rental, delivery, pickup and disposal into explicit lines on the worksheet. No unexplained lump sums.
- **Labor can be in worker-days.** When the estimator says "five guys for two days", enter it as 10 worker-days at $400 each ($50/hr x 8 hr, the same basis as the $1,600 4-man crew-day). Show the math in the description, e.g. "Labor - 5 workers x 2 days".
- **Equipment delivery and pickup are separate charges.** A 15 ft scissor lift was $115 for one day plus $150 delivery and $150 pickup ($415 total; the estimators rounded the allowance to $400-$450). Always ask whether delivery and pickup apply.
- **Leave a cushion on duration, and say so.** On Montebello the first guess (5 workers x 1 week, 3 trucks, $500 fuel) was judged too much. A comparable job suggested 5 workers x 1 day; the estimators settled on 5 workers x 2 days to leave a cushion. When the estimator adds a cushion, note it in the estimate so a reviewer can see why.
- **Comps check the numbers; they don't replace them.** Use past jobs to test whether the crew-days and loads are reasonable. The scope quantities and the estimator's crew/haul inputs still set the cost.

### Price and markup
- **Say which markup formula you used.** The engine uses markup on cost: price = cost x (1 + markup). Gross margin is (price - cost) / price. They are different numbers for the same bid (140% markup = 58% margin). When an estimator gives a percentage out loud, confirm which one they mean before using it, and always report both.
- **Never treat a spoken or transcribed price as confirmed.** Prices from meeting notes or transcripts can be misheard or lose a digit. Keep cost and price as separate raw figures, check that they reconcile (cost x (1 + markup) = price, price - cost = profit), and ask the estimator to confirm against the proposal or job costing sheet before using the price.
- **Ask instead of guessing.** Device counts, access, disposal volume, ceiling height and the final price are the usual gaps. Ask in one round.

### Quantities and scope (estimating principles)
- **Cross-check takeoff quantities against plan dimensions.** Never trust a scale-based takeoff alone. On the parking-lot job the takeoff read about 241,000 SF while the plan dimensions pointed to roughly 1.6 million SF. Check key quantities against written dimensions, the stated lot area, or another independent reference, and flag any big difference as a Quantity question.
- **Demo vs new work.** Before pricing, sort every item into existing work to remove or new work to build. The 24" mat slab on the parking-lot plans was new construction, not demo scope.
- **Use site clues for materials, then flag what is still unclear.** The team decided the parking area was asphalt because of the concrete gutters. When it is still uncertain, flag it for review instead of assuming.
- **Price each scope separately.** On the duplex job, demolition and grading were priced as separate scopes, with extra profit expected on grading. Never roll demo and grading into one unexplained total.
- **Ask when the plans don't say.** If it is unclear what is being removed, where the material goes, or what work is included, flag the gap and ask.

### Estimating workflow
1. Identify the project, plan set, relevant sheets and requested scope.
2. Pull dimensions and quantities from the plans, recording the sheet or source for each.
3. Cross-check key quantities with written dimensions, stated lot area or another independent reference.
4. Classify materials and work: asphalt vs concrete, building vs hardscape, demo vs new work, above-grade vs below-grade, grading vs export/import.
5. List assumptions, exclusions, ambiguities and questions for the estimator or client.
6. Build the cost from labor, equipment, hauling, fuel and other job-specific costs.
7. Apply the chosen pricing strategy and write a clear proposal description.
8. Ask for human review on large discrepancies, uncertain scope, unusual jobs or unsupported production assumptions.

### What to present for every estimate
- Scope included
- Quantity and unit
- Source / plan reference
- Labor and duration assumptions
- Equipment, hauling and other cost assumptions
- Estimated cost
- Markup or pricing method
- Proposed bid
- Exclusions, uncertainties and questions
- Items requiring human approval

### Pricing strategy
- **Keep three numbers apart: cost, price at markup, and final bid.** The estimator may bid below (or above) the markup price to stay competitive. Show all three and the effective markup the final bid works out to.
- **A fixed dollar add is also a pricing method.** On the duplex the team added $15,000 to cost instead of using a percentage. Record it as stated and report the effective markup it works out to.

### Excavation, grading and hauling
- **Plan-reading and takeoff results are not authoritative.** That includes STACK and the bot's own reading of the plans. Verify dimensions and quantities against the source plans.
- **Basement excavation: show the chain.** Footprint, area, depth, volume, then loads. Record the sheet for each number. Load counts are project-specific estimates (the Oct 2026 basement job was about 12 ft deep and estimated at 240-275 super ten loads); never reuse one job's loads-per-volume as a standard conversion.
- **Confirm the big cost drivers before finalizing.** Excavation conditions, equipment duration, haul method and crew size move the number the most. Ask for each one; label anything assumed.
- **Subcontracted hauling replaces the standard load cost.** When hauling is subbed at a per-load price, use that price instead of the $850 all-in load, and do not add hauling fuel on top. Job fuel (for A2Z's own equipment) is still its own line.
- **Equipment rates come from a current quote.** Large equipment for long durations should be priced from a current rental quote or A2Z's own cost, not copied from a past job. If the number given doesn't match the rate sheet, ask (see the basement example).
- **Remove lines that don't apply.** Drop allowances that aren't needed (e.g. a breaker/misc allowance when no breaker is expected) instead of leaving them in "just in case". Say what was removed.
- **No saw cutting unless the work needs it.**
- **Include 12" lifts and recompaction** in grading scope when the plans or soils report call for it.

### Bid structure
- **Each scope is its own titled line item with its own price** (e.g. DEMOLITION and BASEMENT EXCAVATION/GRADING). Crews get separate numbers.
- **Line items must add up exactly to the total bid.** Reconcile quantities, costs, line items, markup and total before producing a bid. The engine rejects scope prices that don't add up.
- **Markup can differ by scope.** A fixed dollar amount on one scope (e.g. about $65,000 added to grading) is as valid as a percentage. Never assume a standard markup from any one example.
- **Scope wording comes from the plans or A2Z's approved wording**, not invented. Keep to what the documents show.

### Keep job-specific numbers separate from rules
Rates, load counts, markups and prices in the Examples below belong to those jobs only. They are evidence and test cases, not defaults. Reusable rules live in the General lessons above; standard rates live only in `data/rates.json`.

## Examples

### Montebello strip-mall interior demo with ceiling tiles (training session, Oct 2026)
- **Status: NOT in the comp library.** The final price is not confirmed. Add it to `data/job_library.json` only after Jesse confirms the sent price from the proposal or job costing sheet.
- Location: strip-mall suite in Montebello, CA, near Beverly Wilcox Village, address number 2242 (verify exact address).
- Access: single man door.
- Area: about 1,079 SF.
- Scope: remove marked walls and doors; remove carpet tile and rubber base; remove suspended ACT ceiling tiles and leave the grid; remove ceiling lights, grilles/diffusers, speakers, security devices and one unclear device ("motion ejectors/injectors", confirm); remove one small (about 24") cabinet.
- Inputs the estimators settled on: 5 workers x 2 days (10 worker-days, includes a cushion), 1 truck load, one 15 ft scissor lift for 1 day with delivery and pickup (about $400-$450 allowance).
- Figures in the transcript: cost $7,450; price heard as "1,780"; "just over $10,000" profit; "140% markup". Prices of $16,000-$18,000 were discussed earlier and one line sounded like "17,800".
- Reconciliation: $7,450 x 2.40 (140% markup on cost) = $17,880, which leaves $10,430 profit ("just over $10,000"). So **$17,880 is the most likely intended price**, with "1,780" a dropped digit. This is a reading of the transcript, not a confirmed price.
- Open question on cost: the settled inputs (10 worker-days x $400 = $4,000, 1 load = $850, lift about $415) come to about $5,265 before fuel, which is well under $7,450. The $7,450 may include other items or an earlier version of the inputs. Confirm against the job costing sheet.

### Parking-lot asphalt demo (training session, Oct 2026) - test case
- Scope settled on: saw-cut, break and haul 2,800 SF of asphalt per plans (70 ft x 40 ft).
- Takeoff discrepancy: scale-based takeoff about 241,000 SF vs roughly 1.6 million SF from plan dimensions. Both are far from the 2,800 SF scope that was priced, which shows why quantities must be traced to a dimension on the plans.
- Excluded: 24" mat slab (new work, not demo).
- Material: asphalt, judged from the concrete gutters.
- Inputs discussed: 3 workers x 3 days (9 worker-days), about a 20K excavator, full-size skid steer, breaker, 10 loads, fuel.
- Price: cost + 140% = $35,760, so cost = $14,900. Bid set at **$29,800** to stay competitive. That is exactly cost x 2.0: effective markup 100%, margin 50%.
- What the bot must do: show the math, surface the takeoff discrepancy, and keep cost ($14,900), price at 140% ($35,760) and final bid ($29,800) as separate numbers.

### Basement excavation + demolition bid (training session, Oct 2026) - test case
